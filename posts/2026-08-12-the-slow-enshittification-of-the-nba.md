---
title: The Slow Enshittification of the NBA
date: 8/12/2026
---
There was a time when following the NBA was simple. You knew which channel to turn to. You could scrape together enough cash for nosebleed seats without missing rent. The team in your city belonged, in some intangible but real sense, to your city. None of that is true anymore. The reasons trace back to a familiar pattern: a product that once served fans first is now being optimized, layer by layer, for the people who own it, broadcast it, and speculate on it. That's the platform-economy phenomenon known as enshittification, when a system built for users gets re-engineered to extract more value from them until the experience degrades and the money flows uphill.

The NBA didn't arrive here by accident. It got here through decisions made by owners, broadcasters, and league executives optimizing for quarterly returns and franchise valuations, not for the person buying a ticket or flipping on a game after work.

## Ownership as an Asset Class, Not a Civic Trust

Sports franchises used to change hands occasionally, usually because a family fortune ran dry or an owner simply wanted out. Now they turn over like real estate in a bull market. The Boston Celtics, the NBA's most decorated franchise, sold last year for a then-record $6.1 billion to a private equity figure, closing barely a year after the team won its 18th championship. That record didn't last long. Months later, the Los Angeles Lakers sold at a $10 billion valuation. Then, within roughly a year of that deal closing, the Lakers sold again, this time for $12.5 billion, breaking not just the NBA record but the record for any professional sports franchise in North American history.

Two sales of the league's flagship franchise inside twelve months tell you what NBA ownership has become. Not a long-term stewardship of a civic institution, but a financial instrument traded among billionaires, private equity funds, and media moguls looking for an asset class immune to normal market cycles. When a team can double in value in a year without adding a single banner, the price tag has stopped reflecting anything about the product on the floor. It reflects leverage, scarcity, and the fact that ultra-wealthy buyers now see franchises the way they see rare art or trophy real estate: appreciating collateral that happens to come with courtside seats.

Fans who've spent decades buying jerseys and season tickets have no say in any of this. They watch their emotional investment get flipped between spreadsheets, sometimes twice in the same calendar year, while ticket prices climb to justify whatever the new owner just paid.

## A TV Deal Built for Shareholders, Not Viewers

In 2024, the NBA signed an 11-year, $76 billion media rights deal with Disney, NBC, and Amazon, ending its 40-year relationship with Turner Sports and TNT in the process. The split was contentious enough that Warner Bros. Discovery threatened legal action, arguing the league had misread its own matching rights. The financial windfall was undeniable. The result for the person trying to watch a Tuesday night game has been closer to chaos.

Under the new arrangement, a single week of NBA action can be scattered across ESPN, ABC, NBC, Peacock, and Amazon Prime Video. In practice that means at least two separate streaming subscriptions plus a cable or antenna setup just to see everything the league offers. A fan who once flipped between two familiar channels now needs to check which app carries their team on a given night, and whether their local Regional Sports Network survived the broader collapse of the RSN model. League and network executives call this maximizing reach. Fans and longtime broadcasters, including Hall of Famer Charles Barkley, have called it a mess. The deal wasn't designed to make basketball easier to find. It was designed to squeeze the largest possible bid out of three competing media giants, and the fragmentation fans now navigate is the direct byproduct of that leverage play.

## Saudi Money Circling the Sport

The NBA has so far avoided the kind of direct sovereign wealth takeover that transformed golf and reshaped European soccer, but the pressure is building at the edges. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, the same fund behind LIV Golf's disruption of the PGA Tour, has been named as a strategic backer of a proposed $5 billion global basketball league spanning Europe and Asia, with reported involvement from LeBron James's longtime business partner Maverick Carter and Sela, a PIF-owned events company. Qatar's sovereign fund has been in discussions around a potential NBA Europe venture through its ownership of Paris Saint-Germain, and has already taken an ownership stake tied to NBA star Kevin Durant.

None of this guarantees a hostile takeover of the NBA itself, but it doesn't have to. The mere threat of a state-backed rival league with essentially unlimited capital is already reshaping incentives. Polling suggests most fans expect the NBA would lose stars to a Saudi-backed competitor offering the kind of nine-figure guaranteed money that upended golf overnight. Just as LIV Golf forced the PGA Tour to abandon decades of tradition and eventually negotiate a merger, the threat of Gulf-state basketball money is pressuring the NBA to protect its market position rather than its fans, reinforcing a business-first mindset at every level of the league.

## Pricing Out the People Who Built the Fanbase

The clearest evidence of enshittification is what it now costs to simply show up. League-wide, the average get-in ticket price for the 2025-26 season sits around $53, but that number hides enormous disparities. Knicks tickets average over $200, nearly four times the league median, and a family of four attending a Knicks game now spends more than $800 once parking, concessions, and fees are included. Teams that had a good season see prices spike to match. Oklahoma City's average ticket price jumped roughly 92% after their 2025 championship run, turning a blue-collar basketball town into one of the pricier tickets in the league almost overnight.

The postseason is worse. NBA Finals tickets now regularly average north of $1,000, and single games have drawn resale demand exceeding some Super Bowls. A trip to the Finals in a market like New York can now cost more than the equivalent seat at the biggest event in American sports. The NBA built its cultural relevance on being more accessible than the NFL: smaller arenas, closer seats, a rawer connection between star players and the crowd. The pricing trajectory has quietly severed that connection. The people who can afford courtside seats increasingly overlap with the people buying entire franchises, while the fans who grew up idolizing these teams get squeezed toward a $20 nosebleed seat, if they can get into the building at all.

## Where This Leaves the Fan

These trends reinforce each other. A league flipped between billionaire ownership groups needs ever-higher revenue to justify ever-higher purchase prices. A fractured, streaming-heavy TV deal generates that revenue by selling access piecemeal to the highest bidders instead of bundling it simply for viewers. Sovereign wealth funds circling the sport add pressure to chase short-term dollars over long-term fan loyalty. Ticket prices climb because, in a league now run like a portfolio of appreciating assets, every seat is inventory priced at whatever the market, increasingly corporate and increasingly wealthy, will bear.

The NBA isn't dying, and the sport on the floor remains extraordinary. But the experience of being an ordinary fan, someone who wants to know what channel the game is on and afford a seat to watch it live, keeps losing out to the priority of maximizing value for owners, broadcasters, and now sovereign investors treating basketball as another line in a global portfolio. It's what happens when short-term financial optimization runs unchecked in a business that used to understand its product was, at its core, a relationship with the people in the seats.
