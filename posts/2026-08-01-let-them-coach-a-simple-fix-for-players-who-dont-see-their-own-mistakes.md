---
title: "Let Them Coach: A Simple Fix for Players Who Don't See Their Own Mistakes"
date: 8/1/2026
---
**When Repetition Alone Stops Working**

If you coach high school basketball, you know the sound of your own voice on repeat. Box out. Make the easy pass. Stop over-dribbling. You've said these things a hundred times this season, and somewhere around repetition one trillion, your players stopped hearing it. Not because they don't care (probably), but because the words have become background noise. You've gone full Charlie Brown teacher voice on them.

What so many coaches don't understand is that this isn't a discipline issue. It's a basic feature of how people process information. Verbal correction is a passive input. Players hear it, nod or say "yes, coach," and move on without ever having to organize the idea for themselves. Compare that to the alternative: put them in charge of explaining the concept to four teammates.

This is sometimes called the protégé effect: people who prepare to teach material process it more deeply than people who are simply told it. Teaching forces you to organize the information, spot the gaps in your own understanding, and fix them before you open your mouth, building stronger memory traces than passively receiving the same information. Interestingly, research on the phenomenon has found that it doesn't even require a full lesson to kick in. Simply expecting to teach material afterward has been shown to boost learning, before any actual teaching happens.

Translate that to a gym: a player who's told to box out fifty times has been told. A player who has to stand at the front of a five-minute walkthrough and explain box-out technique to their teammates, including what happens when they don't do it, has learned it.

**What Happens to the Player-Coach**

They start seeing the game the way you do(and sometimes see things you don't see). A player who has to draw up a defensive rotation has to think about where five bodies need to be, not just where they need to be. That's a completely different cognitive task than executing a set, and it's much closer to what you do every day on the sideline.

They develop real empathy for the frustration of coaching. Once they've tried it, and watched it fall apart because someone blew an assignment, they understand your sideline frustration in a way that no lecture or punitive action could teach them. 

They notice things you've been missing. Players see teammates' habits from angles you don't have. A guard might clock that a teammate's defender is always sagging off in ball-screen coverage, something you may not catch from the sideline every single rep. Letting them coach surfaces that information.

**The Case for Handing Over Control**

This isn't just intuition. It lines up with a growing body of coaching research on athlete-centered and autonomy-supportive coaching. Autonomy-supportive coaching gives athletes real input into decisions, rather than positioning them purely as recipients of instruction. Recent work in sport psychology has found that this kind of coaching climate is tied to greater resilience, optimism, and longer-term athlete development, largely because it creates a psychologically supportive environment rather than one where athletes are simply steered at every turn.

There's also a well-established framework in sport development research called the "4 Cs": competence, confidence, connection, and character. It describes what good coaching should build in athletes beyond wins and losses. On this model, effective coaching draws on professional, interpersonal, and personal knowledge to build all four qualities in athletes over time. Handing a player the clipboard touches all four at once. Competence, because they have to actually understand the concept. Confidence, because they're trusted to run it. Connection, because they're working directly with teammates instead of just receiving orders. Character, because they're accountable when it doesn't go well.

**A few guidelines to keep this useful instead of chaotic:**

Start small. Give a player one possession or one set, not the whole practice. A five-minute segment is enough to create the effect without derailing your plan.

Rotate it. Don't just hand it to your best player or your most vocal one. The quiet sophomore who's never been asked to lead often benefits the most, and shows you something about their basketball IQ you didn't know was there.

Let it fail. The instinct will be to jump in the second the play breaks down. Don't. The mistake, and the player-coach having to call it out or adjust, is the whole point.

Debrief after, not during. Once the possession or segment ends, ask the player-coach what they saw. What broke down? What would they change? This is where the real teaching happens, in their own words, not yours.

Use it as a diagnostic, not just a teaching tool. Watch who thrives with the clipboard. Players who can organize four teammates under pressure are often your best candidates for on-court leadership roles down the line.

Keep it low-stakes. This works best in practice, not in a fourth-quarter timeout in a close game. Save the live experiment for controlled reps where a bad call doesn't cost you a possession that matters.

You can only say "box out" so many times before it stops landing. Handing a player the clipboard doesn't replace your voice. It makes your voice land better, because now they've had to do the thing you've been asking them to understand. They'll walk away seeing their teammates' habits more clearly, and they'll walk away with a little more respect for the coach who's been saying "box out" or "run the play" all season.
